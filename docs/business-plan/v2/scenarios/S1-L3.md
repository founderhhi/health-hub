# Scenario 1 - Level 3 (Fully Funded / Corporate) — Fully Self-Funded

## Scenario Summary

Maximum-investment, zero-dilution path. All capital comes from founders at corporate-level resourcing (~640 productive technical hrs/mo). Every phase runs at or near the shortest feasible duration. Full staffing at every stage. No investor dilution, but the founder capital requirement ($250,000-340,000) raises a fundamental question: founders who can self-deploy this level of capital without external participation are either exceptionally well-capitalized or making an irrational capital allocation decision. This scenario is flagged as **logically incoherent** in the scenario comparison matrix (Document 14).

- Economic cost to Production: **$295,000 / INR 24,485,000**
- Target timeline to Production: **18 months**
- Founder capital required: **$295,000 / INR 24,485,000**
- Investor capital: **$0**
- Equity: 60 / 20 / 0 (20% investor pool reserved, unissued)
- Main risk to watch: logical incoherence — founders with $295,000+ should be attracting investors easily; choosing not to suggests either a deeper problem or an irrational preference for control over capital efficiency

## Key Risks

| # | Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|------|-----------|--------|------------|
| 1 | **Logical incoherence of the scenario** | N/A (structural) | High | Founders with $295,000+ to deploy should attract investors easily. If they cannot, it signals a problem with the business thesis, not a capital structure choice. If they choose not to, they are concentrating risk irrationally. | 2 | **Over-resourcing before product-market fit** | High | High | Corporate-level spend ($22,000-30,000/mo) before confirming PMF means capital is consumed faster than learning accumulates. If Pilot 1 or Pilot 2 reveals a need to pivot, the sunk cost is much higher than in L1 or L2. |
| 3 | **Founder capital concentration risk** | High | Critical | $295,000 in a single pre-revenue venture is a poor risk-adjusted allocation. Founders should only proceed if this represents <20% of their liquid net worth. |
| 4 | **Speed masking operational unreadiness** | Medium | High | The 18-month timeline is aggressive. Moving fast through phases risks launching with inadequate operational maturity (SOPs not battle-tested, staff not fully trained, partner relationships not deep enough). |
| 5 | **Opportunity cost of capital** | Medium | Medium | $295,000 deployed over 18 months with modest ROI could have been invested in diversified assets or used to attract co-investors who share the risk. The capital efficiency of S1-L3 is the worst in the matrix on a risk-adjusted basis. |

## Recommendation

**Avoid (logically incoherent).** S1-L3 represents the structurally contradictory combination of maximum capital deployment with zero external participation. Document 14 flags this scenario explicitly:

> "Founders who can self-fund $250,000-340,000 should be attracting investors easily. If they cannot, it signals a deeper problem. If they choose not to, the capital would be more efficiently deployed with investor participation (shared risk)."

This path exists in the matrix for completeness but should not be pursued because:

1. **If founders have $295,000+ available:** They should pursue S3-O2-I2 or S2-O2-I2, deploying $53,000-95,000 of personal capital and raising investor capital for the rest. This preserves the majority of their personal wealth while achieving similar or faster timelines.
2. **If founders want zero dilution at all costs:** S1-L2 achieves the same goal at $175,000 — $120,000 less capital at risk — with only a 6-month longer timeline.
3. **If founders want the fastest timeline:** S3-O2-I3 reaches Production in 16-20 months with only $53,000-65,000 of founder capital. The remaining capital comes from an investor who shares the risk.

The only scenario where S1-L3 makes sense is if founders have deep personal wealth (>$1.5M liquid), have a specific reason to exclude investors (e.g., strategic secrecy in a competitive market), and view the $295,000 as immaterial relative to their portfolio. Even then, the capital efficiency argument favors bringing in a strategic investor.
